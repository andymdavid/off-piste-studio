## A name can be available and still be a poor choice

Your preferred name comes back green in a register check. The domain is free. The designer is ready to start. Then somebody finds a similar trade mark, an established overseas business or a namesake with poor reviews.

Finding that conflict after registration, design and launch makes every decision more expensive. It can affect packaging, signage, contracts, websites, email addresses and the way buyers find the business.

Each availability result clears one question. ASIC tests whether a name can be registered under its rules. IP Australia records trade marks. Domain registries license domains under their own requirements. Search results show the public evidence already attached to a word or phrase.

The practical goal is to make a documented decision before commitment. You may proceed, revise the name, investigate a concern with a qualified adviser or reject the candidate. That decision needs evidence from every system that matters to the intended use.

## Decide what the name needs to do

A candidate only makes sense in context. Record the exact spelling, intended goods or services, customer group, operating locations and likely expansion. Note whether it will be a registered company name, business name, public brand, product name or some combination of those roles.

Also define the organisation behind it. A standalone venture has different naming needs from a subsidiary, endorsed brand or division. If several entities will share public evidence, decide how to [structure the relationship between the parent company and brand](/insights/parent-company-subsidiary-brand-entity-search-guide) before judging the name in isolation.

Keep spelling variants and abbreviations in the record. A name that only works when buyers reproduce unusual punctuation or spacing may be harder to recognise consistently across platforms. This is a commercial observation to test with the audience, not a registration rule.

## Check the Australian registers

Run the exact candidate through the relevant ASIC checks and record the result and date. ASIC applies defined rules to business names, including whether a proposed name is identical or nearly identical to an existing registered name. Its [rules for acceptable business names](https://www.asic.gov.au/for-business-and-companies/business-names/register-a-business-name/rules-for-acceptable-business-names) explain the register test and restrictions.

Company names have their own process. ASIC's [company name availability guidance](https://asic.gov.au/for-business/registering-a-company/steps-to-register-a-company/company-name-availability/) explains how to check and, where needed, reserve a proposed company name.

Treat a green or available result as evidence about registration. Exclusive use, trade mark clearance and market confusion require separate checks. ASIC can permit names that remain commercially similar, and ASIC itself separates name registration from trade mark protection. The Australian Government's [business naming guidance](https://business.gov.au/planning/new-businesses/choose-a-business-name) also directs businesses to check names, trade marks, websites, domains and social channels separately.

Save the exact result rather than writing only “ASIC checked”. If the outcome requires review, assign an owner and keep the candidate unresolved until the response is clear.

## Search trade marks in the right classes

Search the exact wording, close spellings, phonetic variants and distinctive words. Then consider the goods and services attached to potentially similar marks. IP Australia explains that a mark may be too similar when it relates to the same or similar goods or services and may cause confusion. Its [trade mark search guidance](https://www.ipaustralia.gov.au/trade-marks/search-existing-trade-marks) provides TM Checker for an initial idea check and Australian Trade Mark Search for more advanced research.

A self-service search is screening evidence, not legal clearance. Similarity, distinctiveness, ownership and classification can require judgement that a tool result cannot provide. If the search finds a similar mark, the planned investment is material or the team wants advice about infringement or protection, ask a qualified trade mark professional or lawyer.

Don't narrow the search to the class number you first expect to use. Describe the actual offer and likely expansion, then check that the proposed classes match it. An incomplete description can make a clean-looking result falsely reassuring.

## Check domains and social profiles

Check the domains the business genuinely expects buyers to use, including relevant .au namespaces and obvious misspellings. Use the [.au WHOIS and registration guidance](https://www.auda.org.au/au-domain-names/learn-about-au-domain-names/build-your-online-presence/how-to-register-a-au-domain-name/) to confirm availability and the current eligibility and allocation requirements for the selected namespace.

A free domain confers no rights in the name. A registered business name or trade mark also leaves domain availability subject to a separate system. Record availability, public registration information, eligibility and any practical acquisition question independently.

Check the social profiles the business will actually maintain. An unused handle on one platform doesn't offset a well-established namesake elsewhere. Look at near matches, spelling variants and whether a profile's history or audience could create confusion.

The policy position can change. As at 22 October 2026, auDA had announced public consultation on proposed licensing-rule updates and stated that no final decisions had been made. Recheck [auDA's current licensing-rules update](https://www.auda.org.au/news-insights/statements/update-on-au-licensing-rules-review/) immediately before relying on eligibility or allocation requirements.

## Read the search results as a buyer would

Run a dated, signed-out search where practical for the exact name, spaced and unspaced versions, common misspellings, the name with the service, the name with the location and the name with “reviews”. Repeat the checks in the markets the business expects to serve.

Record what is visible rather than assigning an invented uniqueness score. Note established organisations, dominant meanings, adjacent categories, reputation spillover and how often a buyer would need extra context to find the intended business. Search results observed on one date are point-in-time evidence, not a forecast.

Google says its site names are generated automatically from homepage content and references elsewhere on the web. Its [site-name guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/site-names) recommends a unique, consistent and non-misleading name, while making clear that the displayed name remains automated. A clean shortlist won't guarantee a particular search label. It can reduce avoidable ambiguity before the web fills with mixed evidence.

## Look for entity collisions before they harden

A collision becomes more important when the other entity has strong, repeated references across directories, articles, profiles and reviews. Those sources can give buyers, search engines and AI systems several plausible meanings for the same name.

Review the other entity's category, geography and audience. Two businesses can coexist with similar wording in unrelated markets, but distance alone may not resolve a collision when both sell online or use the same platforms. This is where the broader model of [how search and AI systems understand a business](/insights/how-ai-search-understands-your-business) helps. Identity is reinforced by agreement among owned information, profiles, public records and third-party sources.

Keep the boundary clear. You are observing public evidence and assessing practical risk. You aren't predicting a platform's behaviour or deciding legal rights. If the business has already launched and systems are mixing it with a namesake, use the guide to [repair an existing business-name collision](/insights/fix-ai-search-business-name-confusion-guide).

## Make and document the decision

Bring the findings onto two axes. One captures registry or trade mark conflict, including unresolved formal questions. The other captures practical search collision risk from established names, dominant meanings and confusing public evidence. These axes produce four distinct decision areas, but they don't create a legal opinion or numeric score.


Proceed means the checks found low conflict evidence and the team accepts the remaining uncertainty. Revise means a clearer spelling, qualifier or different candidate may resolve practical ambiguity, subject to running every check again. Investigate means an unresolved registry or trade mark issue needs authoritative review. Reject means serious formal concerns and high search collision risk make further brand investment hard to justify.

For every candidate, retain the sources checked, date, observed conflict, commercial consequence, unresolved question, owner and decision. Write down the reason. A later challenge is easier to handle when the team can see what it knew and why it proceeded.
