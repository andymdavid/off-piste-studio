## A complete handoff reduces editing before drafting

A familiar content problem starts with a short instruction such as “write an article about AI content briefs”. The resulting draft may be fluent, yet it has guessed the reader, argument, evidence, examples, boundaries, and next action. An editor then spends more time rebuilding the strategy than improving the writing.

The risk is bigger than bland prose. A model may receive confidential details that shouldn't enter the tool. A writer may repeat a claim that nobody has verified. An expert may arrive at the final review and discover that the article takes a position they wouldn't support.

Google's [people-first content guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) asks publishers to consider audience, original value, expertise, sourcing, authorship, and the reason content exists. NIST's [Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf) describes controls around source verification, provenance, testable requirements, and human oversight. A practical brief turns those broad expectations into decisions a content team can inspect.

The broader [seven-stage AI-assisted content workflow](/insights/ai-assisted-content-workflow-expertise-guide) covers topic selection through publication and measurement. This guide focuses on making one approved assignment ready for a human writer or an AI-assisted drafting step.

## The brief and source pack do different jobs

Off Piste treats the brief, source pack, prompt, draft, and publication audit as separate controls.

The strategy brief records the reader, intent, argument, page role, original contribution, boundaries, voice references, owners, and definition of done. The source pack records the evidence and expert material that support particular claims. A prompt gives a drafting tool a bounded task using the approved parts of those records. The draft is the output to review. The publication audit tests that finished output before it goes live.

Collapsing everything into one enormous prompt makes important records harder to maintain. A source file, permission decision, interview transcript, or approved claim may need to stay in a governed location even when a short extract can safely inform the prompt. The source of truth should remain outside a chat history.

This separation is Off Piste's editorial operating model. It gives each decision an owner and lets the team update evidence or permissions without rewriting the whole assignment.

## Start with the reader job and page role

Name the primary reader and the moment that brought them to the page. “Small business owners” is too broad. “A marketing lead with an approved topic who needs to brief a writer” gives the article something concrete to do.

Then record the decision the page should support, the searches that express that need, and the role this URL plays beside existing content. The [search intent and decision-stage method](/insights/search-intent-decision-stage-content-guide) helps when the team hasn't yet agreed on those fields.

Add the original contribution before drafting. It might be a first-party example, a practical synthesis of several standards, an expert's counterexample, or a template that makes the method usable. Google's guidance on [using generative AI content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content) emphasises accuracy, quality, relevance, and added value. It also recommends useful context about how content was created when readers would reasonably expect it.

A real reader job also protects against publishing for volume alone. Google's [scaled content abuse policy](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies#scaled-content) focuses on large amounts of unoriginal content created primarily to manipulate rankings, whether people, automation, or both produced it. Scale itself isn't the test. The brief needs to show why this particular page deserves to exist for its audience.

## Write the argument before the outline

Write one sentence that states the central claim. For this article, it's that a writer-ready handoff must supply strategy, evidence, expertise, permissions, ownership, and a test for completion before drafting begins.

Next, record the questions the page must answer and what the reader should understand by the end. Only then build the outline. Each section should advance the central claim rather than collect adjacent facts.

Out-of-scope notes matter too. This page doesn't need to own the broad AI content workflow, reproduce a complete voice system, perform the final quality audit, or settle every disclosure decision. Those jobs already belong to focused resources. Explicit boundaries reduce repetition and help neighbouring pages retain a distinct search role.

## Build a claim-level source pack

A source pack connects each material claim to evidence that supports its exact scope.

For each claim, record the proposed wording, source name, direct URL, source tier, publication or update date, access date, supported scope, required qualification, status, evidence owner, and review trigger. NIST's generative AI profile recommends reviewing and verifying sources and citations, documenting provenance, defining testable requirements, and assigning human oversight. The claim record adapts those controls to everyday editorial work.

Use a small status vocabulary. Approved means the evidence supports the wording. Qualified means the claim can proceed with a stated limit. Replace means the source is weak or mismatched. Create first-party evidence means the business needs its own observation, analysis, or record. Hold means the claim can't safely move forward yet.

The [website source evaluation method](/insights/evaluate-sources-website-content-guide) helps assess authority, method, freshness, scope, and claim fit. Higher-risk marketing wording needs the more detailed [claim substantiation workflow](/insights/website-claim-substantiation-workflow), including approved language, qualifications, ownership, and review triggers.

## Capture expert judgement and first-party knowledge

Sources can establish facts. They can't supply your team's delivery experience, judgement, or commercial position unless that knowledge is recorded.

Name the subject-matter expert and capture the interview notes, first-party examples, observed patterns, counterexamples, proprietary methods, unresolved questions, and required approval. Mark which material may be quoted, paraphrased, anonymised, or kept internal. Google's people-first framework calls attention to who created the content, how it was produced, why it exists, and whether it demonstrates first-hand expertise.

The expert contribution should change the article. It might narrow a recommendation, explain when a common tactic fails, or show how a decision works in practice. If the source pack contains only public summaries, a polished draft can still lack a reason to come from this business.

Voice controls should point to a maintained system rather than a few vague adjectives. Use an [AI ready brand voice system](/insights/ai-ready-brand-voice-guide-content-system) for approved examples, observable rules, exclusions, and test cases, then reference the relevant version in the brief.

## Set privacy and permission boundaries before prompting

Record the approved tool, account or workspace, permitted data classes, excluded personal or confidential information, source-file location, copyright and reuse permissions, retention considerations, and escalation owner before any material is uploaded.

The OAIC's [guidance on commercially available AI products](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products) recommends due diligence on how a product handles data, meaningful human oversight, and controls that prevent personal information being entered into publicly available generative AI tools. The exact obligations depend on the organisation, information, activity, and applicable law. Check the current product terms and workspace settings as part of the assignment rather than relying on an old assumption.

Sensitive records may be referenced in the governed pack while remaining outside the prompt. An editor can provide an approved, de-identified summary when the underlying file isn't suitable for the drafting tool. The wider [AI governance policy checklist](/insights/ai-governance-policy-checklist-growing-businesses) helps establish organisation-level tool approval, data rules, ownership, and escalation.

## Make the handoff testable

Assign the writer, subject-matter expert, editor, and publisher. One person may hold several roles in a small team, though the responsibility should still be explicit.

Acceptance checks should cover factual support, required qualifications, expert approval, voice references, required and prohibited claims, internal links, AI contribution, the disclosure decision, and the final audit. NIST's emphasis on testable requirements and human oversight is useful here. The Off Piste interpretation is to turn “make it good” into conditions a reviewer can actually verify.

The brief reduces ambiguity while human verification remains essential. Open the sources, inspect the draft, and route the finished article through the [AI-assisted content quality audit](/insights/ai-assisted-content-quality-audit-guide). Record the intended AI contribution and use the [AI content disclosure guide](/insights/ai-content-disclosure-guide-business) when the team needs to decide whether readers need additional context.

## See one claim move through the handoff

Consider a fictional Off Piste assignment about preparing an AI content brief. One proposed claim says an organisation should stop staff from placing personal information into publicly available generative AI tools.

The source record names the OAIC guidance, links directly to it, records an access date of 12 October 2026, and marks it as a primary Australian regulator source. Its supported scope is organisational privacy practice in Australia. The qualification notes that applicable obligations vary with the organisation, information, activity, and law. The evidence owner is the editor, with a review trigger when the OAIC guidance or the selected product's terms change.

The drafting instruction reads: explain why the brief needs excluded-input and approved-tool fields, cite the OAIC guidance, retain the qualification, and avoid giving legal advice. The expert note adds that governed source records may stay outside the prompt while an approved de-identified summary is used. The internal link points to the broader AI governance checklist. The reviewer must open the guidance, check the final wording against its scope, and confirm the current product terms before approval.

Only after those records are complete does a short prompt become useful:

> Draft the privacy-boundaries section from the approved brief. Use only the supplied OAIC claim record and expert note. Preserve the qualification, link the named guidance contextually, and flag any missing information instead of inferring it.

The prompt is short because the decisions already exist. The governed pack remains the source of truth for evidence and permissions.

## Check whether the brief is ready

A ready handoff lets the writer identify the reader decision, intended argument, approved evidence, expert contribution, exclusions, link role, owner, reviewer, and acceptance test without inventing missing strategy. The practice check above covers the essential fields. The full template records the detail needed to approve them.

The failed field usually identifies the capability the team needs next. Missing reader intent, evidence strategy, or page ownership points to [SEO and content strategy](/services/seo). Missing positioning or an inconsistent voice points to [brand strategy](/services/branding). A brief that can't move through approvals, metadata, links, or publication may expose a [website and publishing-system problem](/services/website-design).

Resolve those gaps in the brief, then start the draft with a handoff the team can defend.
