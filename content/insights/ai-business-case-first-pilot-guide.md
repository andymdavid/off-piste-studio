## Start with the decision

To fund a promising AI idea, the budget owner needs to know what problem the pilot will address, what it will cost to test, what could go wrong and what evidence would justify another commitment.

Start with one shortlisted workflow. Name the sponsor and the person who can approve spending, the people affected, the pilot boundary and the date for a decision. If you're still choosing between candidates, [prioritise your AI use cases](/insights/ai-use-case-prioritisation-framework-business) first. The business case takes one candidate forward, using forecasts rather than results already achieved.

[HM Treasury's business-case guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/guidance-on-developing-business-cases/guidance-on-developing-business-cases-for-projects-and-programmes) recommends considering options, costs, benefits, risks and delivery arrangements. It's public-sector guidance, not a mandatory process for a private business. Make the case detailed enough for the size and risk of the decision.

## Establish the baseline and alternatives

Before estimating improvement, describe current volume, staff time, quality or rework, direct costs and customer impact. Record where each number came from, which period it covers and any gaps in the evidence. A short sample of time records may be a reasonable starting point, but it should be labelled as a sample rather than a reliable annual forecast.

Compare three feasible choices for the same work over the same period: continue as you are, make a non-AI improvement, or run the AI pilot. The non-AI option might be a better template, clearer routing or a conventional rule. Explain what each option changes and what it leaves in place. If the AI option proceeds, the [build, buy or hybrid delivery guide](/insights/build-buy-hybrid-ai-business-decision-guide) helps explore ownership costs without assuming the first tool is the right one.

## Build a transparent forecast

Suppose a service business wants to help staff prepare responses to routine enquiries. The following figures are entirely illustrative assumptions, not benchmarks or client results. Each option covers 1,000 enquiries over three months. Staff check every response before sending it. The business has no verified reduction in payroll or other cash spending from released time.

Business as usual takes an assumed 12 minutes per enquiry, or 200 staff hours, with no incremental non-labour cost. A non-AI template takes nine minutes per enquiry, or 150 hours, and costs $300 to set up. The central AI pilot takes seven minutes per enquiry including review, or approximately 116.7 handling hours, and costs $2,000 for preparation, integration and licences.

For the central AI case, 1,000 × 7 ÷ 60 = 116.7 hours. Against business as usual, that releases approximately 83.3 hours in handling time. Against the template option, it releases 33.3 handling hours and costs an additional $1,700 in non-labour spending. The AI estimate assumes 10% of enquiries still take 12 minutes without AI and 90% take an average of 6.44 minutes including human review. That gives 0.1 × 12 + 0.9 × 6.44 ≈ 7 minutes. Preparation includes data checking and training. The integration and licence allowance includes pilot-period support. The staff time needed to prepare and train is costed separately below.

Those released hours give the team capacity to handle more work or improve service. To count a cash saving, the business must show an actual avoided expense. Do not count both the value of the same hours and revenue supposedly created by using those hours unless the relationship and costs are explicit. Quality, customer confidence and risk can matter even when they cannot reasonably be priced.

### Test the assumptions that could change the decision

The [HM Treasury guidance on sensitivity analysis](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/guidance-on-developing-business-cases/guidance-on-developing-business-cases-for-projects-and-programmes) describes testing how changes in assumptions affect an appraisal. The scenarios below apply that idea to our invented example. They keep volume and the baseline fixed, vary adoption and reviewed handling time, and include staff setup and training hours separately from the per-enquiry figures.

In the **low scenario**, AI handles 50% of enquiries at nine minutes each, including review. The other half take 12 minutes each. Handling takes 175 hours, plus 30 hours for setup and training. Total effort is 205 hours, five more than the baseline, and non-labour costs are $2,500.

In the **central scenario**, AI handles 90% at 6.44 minutes each, with the remaining 10% taking 12 minutes. Handling takes approximately 116.6 hours and setup and training take 20 hours. Total effort is 136.6 hours, releasing 63.4 hours against the baseline, with $2,000 in non-labour costs.

In the **high scenario**, AI handles all enquiries at five minutes each. Handling takes 83.3 hours and setup and training take 15 hours. Total effort is 98.3 hours, releasing 101.7 hours against the baseline, with $1,800 in non-labour costs.

For example, the low case is (500 × 9 + 500 × 12) ÷ 60 = 175 handling hours. Adding 30 setup hours gives 205, five more than the baseline. The central calculation uses the unrounded 6.44-minute assumption, so its handling total differs slightly from the rounded seven-minute figure above. The template alternative's 150 hours excludes setup staff time. Estimate and add that time before making a final comparison. Likewise, check whether licences, data preparation, integration, training, review and ongoing support are fully included in each option. If ongoing operation continues after the pilot, forecast those costs over a separate, common operating period rather than hiding them in a three-month result.

Some assumptions are less certain than others. Adoption and review time drive this example. A system that produces difficult-to-check drafts can consume more capacity than it releases. Record an evidence source and a confidence level for each material assumption, and identify what the pilot will test. A costed downside can be a sound reason to revise the scope before spending.

## Make risk and dependencies visible

Describe what the system will do, the information it can access, who reviews its output and who could be affected by an error. The voluntary [NIST AI Risk Management Framework Core](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/) calls for documenting context, intended tasks, business value and risk tolerance. Use it to inform the case, not as a certification claim.

For the enquiry example, the operations owner could be responsible for response quality, with a sample checked against approved source material and a stop if inaccurate advice reaches a customer. A data owner could confirm permitted access and remove unnecessary personal information before testing. The trial stops if access or privacy concerns remain unresolved. A delivery owner could document integration and fallback arrangements, with staff returning to the existing process if the tool fails. Name the people responsible and agree acceptable thresholds with them. These examples are starting points, not universal limits.

Use an [AI readiness assessment](/insights/ai-readiness-assessment-growing-business) to challenge assumptions about data, ownership and staff capacity. Budget and schedule the evidence needed for [pre-launch workflow testing](/insights/ai-workflow-pre-launch-testing-guide), including checks for errors, escalation and human review before customer-facing use.

## Request a staged decision

The funding record can fit on a page, with supporting calculations attached. Complete it before asking for approval:

1. **Decision and owner:** Name the budget owner, sponsor and operational owner. State the specific workflow, users and pilot boundary.
2. **Alternatives and forecast:** Summarise the baseline, non-AI option and AI option over the same period. Attach the low, central and high assumptions, costs and non-financial effects.
3. **Commitment:** Request a maximum pilot budget, duration and decision date. Identify who can authorise changes and how much staff time is reserved. For the example, the sponsor might request up to $2,500 in non-labour spending plus 30 staff hours for setup and training over three months. That's an illustrative ceiling, not a recommended budget.
4. **Evidence and risk:** Specify the baseline, sample and owner for measuring handling time and quality. For example, require an average reviewed handling time of no more than seven minutes across an agreed representative sample, with no material customer-facing errors. Agree the sample, error definition and acceptable risk before testing. Stop for a material privacy incident, unsafe output reaching a customer or a forecast overspend without renewed approval.
5. **Next gate:** On the decision date, the budget owner reviews actual spend, staff time, quality, adoption and incidents against the case. Approve a separately costed next phase, revise and retest, or stop. Pilot approval doesn't itself authorise a rollout.

After the trial, [measure actual workflow ROI and reliability](/insights/measure-ai-workflow-automation-roi-reliability) and replace assumptions with observed evidence. If the next commitment is approved, use an [implementation roadmap](/insights/ai-implementation-roadmap-business-guide) to sequence it alongside other work and dependencies.

Proceed when the expected benefit is worth the bounded commitment, the risks are acceptable and the team can produce the evidence for the next decision. If those conditions are missing, narrow the pilot, improve the baseline or choose another option.
